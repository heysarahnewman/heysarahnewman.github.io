Can a bar mitzvah candy toss send someone to the ER? I searched 21 years of injury data.

October 2026

Last month, news that Sunkist Fruit Gems had been discontinued popped up in my family group chat, and my boyfriend and I started talking about the candies people toss at bar and bat mitzvahs. It turned out each of us separately knew a kid who had gotten a corneal abrasion after being hit in the eye with one of those candies during their bar mitzvah. How common could that be?

I went looking for an answer in case-level data from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS), a sample of U.S. emergency departments maintained by the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC). I searched 2005 through 2025. Along the way I looked for any injury narrative that explicitly mentioned a bar or bat mitzvah, and any injuries involving the raised chair during the hora for good measure. The data turned up some memorable cases - and a lesson in the sparsity of ED narrative note data.

First, the candy question

I searched broadly for candy and related words, throwing and impact, and injuries to the eye or face. I reviewed the candidates and kept uncertain records separate.

Zero sample records explicitly connect a candy-related eye injury to a bar or bat mitzvah or an identifiable synagogue candy-throwing custom. Seven records remained in a broad “possible” review group because their narratives do not identify the setting. That group is mixed: some involve a candy wrapper, a lollipop stick, or a bag of candy rather than a Fruit Gem thrown into an eye. They are not seven confirmed mitzvah injuries, and several do not even establish that candy itself was thrown.

One 2025 narrative is promising: a 7-year-old girl was “AT A PARTY” when another child threw a lollipop and hit her eye, causing a corneal abrasion and eyeball hemorrhage. But what kind of party? NEISS doesn't say.

Then I searched for the event itself

So, nothing explicitly describing the "Sunkist to the eye" injury I had in mind. But what about any other injuries involving bar mitzvahs? A direct search found five sample cases whose narratives explicitly mention a bar or bat mitzvah. Here they are, with the original abbreviated ER wording from NEISS preserved:

Year Age What the narrative says Injury
2007 73 “73 YOF FELL WHILE DANCING AT A BAT MITZVAH SUSTAINING A FRACTURED HIP” Hip fracture; admitted to hospital
2008 13 “13 YOF WAS DANCING AT A BAR MITZVAH AND HAD ONLY SOCKS ON, PARTNER HADHARD SHOES ON AND STEPPED ON PT'S RIGHT FOOT CAUSING CRUSH INJURY” Foot crushing injury; treated and released
2014 12 “12 YO FEMALE DANCING AT A BAR MITZVAH AND SOMEONE STEPPED ON PTS FOOT.DX CONTUSION” Foot contusion; treated and released
2016 9 “9 YOM @ BAR MITZVAH AT A RESTUARANT RUNNING W/COUSINS TRIPPED FELL HITFOREHEAD ON CONCRETE STEPS DX FOREHEAD LACERATION” Forehead laceration; treated and released
2022 15 “15 YOF FELL WHILE DANCING AT A BAR MITZVAH. DX CLAVICLE FX” Clavicle fracture; treated and released

Four happened while dancing. Two involved feet being stepped on - youch! The fifth involved a child running with cousins and falling on steps. These five cases answer a very narrow question: How many cases in the NEISS sample explicitly say bar or bat mitzvah? But they don't tell us how many injuries actually occurred at those events nationwide.

What about the chair lift during the hora?

I thought about other events during a bar or bat mitzvah that might lead to injury. I remembered the terror I felt going airborne during my chair lift. So I tried a similar broad search for hora, dancing, weddings, and people being lifted or carried in chairs. No narrative explicitly identified a hora injury. Three sample cases describe an injury to someone lifting or carrying another person in a chair:

  • In 2007, a 15-year-old lifting someone in a chair lost his grip; the chair fell onto his hand, fracturing it. No event is named.
  • In 2012, a 39-year-old carrying a person in a chair above his head twisted and developed lower-back pain and sciatica. No event is named.
  • In 2014, a 30-year-old carrying a person in a chair at a wedding injured his shoulder when the chair came down on it. The NEISS narrative does not say whether this was a hora.

These are strikingly close to the mechanism I was looking for, and all three matched the original CPSC files. They remain possible chair-lift-related cases, not confirmed hora cases. An additional 2009 case describes a large chair coming down on a dancer’s foot, but does not establish that anyone was in or lifting the chair. I kept it separate as an even more ambiguous lead.

Some limitations

NEISS covers product-associated injuries treated in its sampled emergency departments, not every injury or every care setting. Its narratives are short, and narrative length changed in 2019, which can affect how often an event is named. A patient injured during a hora could appear simply as “fell while dancing”; a candy injury at a synagogue could appear with no synagogue mentioned. Conversely, a party with a lollipop injury cannot be assumed to be a bar mitzvah.

So the answer to the question that started this is: I could not identify a confirmed bar or bat mitzvah candy-related eye injury in the NEISS sample. That is not evidence that the two injuries we heard about were rare or did not happen. Five sample narratives explicitly mention a bar or bat mitzvah, but none describes candy in the eye or a confirmed hora chair lift. Broader searches found uncertain candy-related eye cases and plausible chair-lifting injuries without established event settings. The data can reveal individual stories. They cannot tell us how often these particular injuries happen at these particular celebrations.

Other sources?

I investigated the use of other datasets related to injury or emergency room visits. Unfortunately, none were ideal.

  • The National Emergency Medical Services Information System (NEMSIS) captures cause of injury and context, and has a free text narrative field - but that narrative field is not publicly available.
  • The National Hospital Ambulatory Care Survey (NHAMCS) has all reasons for visit, but it's not a verbatim ED note, it's coded into categories. It seems unlikely they created a separate category for "Sunkist to the eye".
  • The NEISS-All Injury Program (NEISS-AIP) captures all injuries, not just ones related to consumer products. But the narratives aren't publicly available.
  • Electronic health record (EHR) data. I don't have access to any - but let me know if you do, I would love to chat!

Methods and data: I searched the 2005–2024 consolidated public NEISS case file assembled from CPSC annual data and CPSC’s 2025 case file, then checked identified records against available CPSC annual files. I kept a case table, the search code, a source manifest, and methods notes for the candy and chair-lift searches documenting their limits. The terms “confirmed” and “possible” refer to whether the event and mechanism are documented in the narrative, not whether the patient’s injury was real.
